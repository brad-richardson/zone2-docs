Build notes

How Zone2 works — and what I learned building it.

A heart-rate-clamped Zone 2 controller for smart trainers, written up in the open. None of the method here is novel; the value was in the execution and the honesty. When I started I couldn’t find a plain account of how to build one of these — so here is mine, with the research, the parameters, an honest negative result, and real sample data.

Single-author side project. This page describes the shipped method; it is not medical advice. Absolute numbers below are one person’s settings, shown to make the design concrete.

The one idea

A normal ERG workout holds power constant. Your body doesn’t cooperate: as you warm up, dehydrate, and fatigue, your heart rate drifts upward at the same watts — cardiac drift — and an “easy” ride quietly becomes a tempo ride. Zone2 inverts the control variable. It holds your heart rate in a band and lets the trainer’s power move underneath it.

Normal ERG locks the watts and lets your effort drift. Zone2 holds the effort and lets the watts move.

This isn’t a compromise — for the Zone 2 goal specifically it’s the more honest signal. When HR and power disagree, heart rate is the better proxy for physiological state, and staying under the first lactate threshold matters more than holding a watts number. Iñigo San Millán, the coach behind the Zone 2 revival, uses HR as the primary metric and accepts declining power; TrainerDay’s HR+ mode implements the same inversion. The Friel-school “power leads, HR informs” reconciles cleanly: for fat-oxidation base work, clamp HR and let power fall. (High North on San Millán, TrainerDay HR modes)

Why it lands with real riders The strongest feedback I got wasn’t about the science. An experienced cyclist who historically disliked Zone 2 — because it was boring and he always pushed too hard — called it the best Zone 2 workout he’d had. The product’s actual job is relief: make the easy ride stay easy without making the rider manage it.

What “a good Zone 2 ride” even is

Before building the controller I had to pin down what it should produce. The short version of the research (full notes):

  • Steady beats undulating. The benefit needs the whole duration in-zone. Surges spike lactate, which suppresses fat oxidation for up to ~30 minutes afterward — so the “Zone 2” workouts full of bursts and tempo bumps optimize engagement, not the stimulus. Zone2’s presets hold one flat band.
  • Duration & frequency. ~45 min minimum to be worth it, 60 min standard, 90+ for trained riders. One continuous session beats the same minutes split in two. 3 days/week improves; more is better up to a point.
  • Little warmup needed. A 5–10 min ramp-in lets HR rise into the band before the clamp engages; a few minutes to spin down.

The control loop

The loop is deliberately simple and slow — a calm hand on the dial, not a twitchy PID. Every tick (1 Hz) it reads HR and, on a slower cadence, nudges the trainer’s target power:

KnobValueWhy
Decision cadenceevery 30 sHR responds to a power change on the order of tens of seconds; reacting faster just chases noise.
HR smoothing15 s windowDecide against smoothed HR, not the latest beat, so one high sample doesn’t yank the trainer.
Step up+4 WWhen HR sits below band, add watts gently.
Step down−8 WAsymmetric on purpose. Over-band must back off faster than under-band ramps up — the failure that matters is going too hard.
Ramp-in starta low floorBegin easy and rise into the band; never start with a jolt.

That asymmetry — shed watts twice as fast as you add them — is the whole temperament of the thing. Under an HR clamp, the direction you never want to be slow about is down.

Safety invariants — the part I’m proudest of

This app actuates hardware attached to a person who is working hard. So the safety rules don’t live in the UI where a refactor can lose them — they live in the portable core, each pinned by a named regression test. The governing principle: every failure resolves toward easy, never toward harder.

InvariantBehavior
HR signal lostThe target decays toward the ramp-in floor (~1 W/s after a short grace) — it never escalates power to “chase” a heart rate it can no longer see.
Implausible HRRaw HR outside 30–220 bpm is dropped at intake; stale packets are rejected by timestamp. (A valid constant BPM is not treated as stuck — steady HR is real.)
Lost skin contactThe strap’s contact bit, debounced ~5 s to ignore jersey flutter, routes into the same ease-down path — earlier and more specific than the stale-signal backstop.
Power ceilingEvery target passes a clamp to the max watts you set; a corrupt config (e.g. min ≥ max, which would invert the clamp into a floor) is rejected on load and falls back to safe defaults.
HR ceiling hard-stopA hard ceiling above your band bypasses the slow loop and latches. It auto-tracks the band (band-top + a fixed margin) so it can’t be accidentally left below the target.

The clock is injected into the core, so all of this is tested in virtual time — a mid-ride disconnect, a half-open link, a suspend/resume — without waiting for real seconds. That’s what let me trust it before pointing it at a trainer with my own legs on it.

Calibration: measuring your easy band with DFA‑a1

You can set the band three ways: from age (≈67–73% of estimated max HR, centre ~70% — the conversational-longevity Zone 2, not the tempo that %-of-max formulas drift into), from threshold HR (80–87% of LTHR when you know it), or by measuring it with a short step test.

The measurement uses DFA‑a1 — the short-scale detrended-fluctuation exponent of your beat-to-beat (R-R) intervals. As you cross the first ventilatory threshold (VT1, the top of Zone 2), heart-rate variability loses its fractal correlation and a1 falls through ~0.75. The step test walks power up in rungs, holding ~90 rpm, and reads the HR at which a1 crosses 0.75.

Nothing here is novel — and that’s the point The a1 = 0.75 aerobic-threshold landmark is Rogers, Gronwald et al. (2021). The idea sits on Gronwald’s and Flatt’s HRV-complexity work, and the whole R-R → a1 pipeline was already in hobbyist tools (FatMaxxer, HRV Logger / Marco Altini) before I touched it. My box-counting matches the reference nolds implementation to ~1e‑15 on public PhysioNet data. I built an implementation, not a discovery.

The two thresholds people conflate

The one subtlety worth stating plainly, because it burned me: there are two different percentages in this pipeline and they are not the same knob.

  • The per-beat artifact rule decides whether an individual R-R interval is a misdetection to correct. I use a median-deviation reject (Lipponen & Tarvainen 2019), and made the tolerance HR-adaptive: loose at low HR / high variability, strict at high HR. Its transition band is 110–145 bpm — the range where a1 actually gets computed for cycling. (The reference apps used 85–90 bpm, which pinned every real riding window to the strict arm; that was wrong for a trainer.)
  • The corrected-fraction quality gate decides whether the whole window is trustworthy, from how many beats had to be corrected: <3% good, 3–5% marginal, >5% unusable → the calibration refuses rather than reporting a confident wrong number.

Rejected beats are interpolated to the local reference rather than dropped, so the DFA profile has no discontinuity. On a real Polar H10 this “auto + interpolate” pipeline is what stopped spurious a1 crashes; the plain fixed-20%-drop default gave false threshold crossings.

What’s validated — and what isn’t

Honesty is a feature here, so the app says exactly this: the box math is validated against a reference on public data; the VT1 read reports its HR with a confidence interval (the a1 uncertainty propagated through the local HR-vs-a1 slope, floored at ±2 bpm). It is biased low — it tends to place VT1 a touch under the true threshold — which is the safe direction for a Zone 2 app: undershooting the easy band costs nothing. What is not claimed: that a single step test is a lab test, or that a “confirmed” read is anything more than a good-quality a1 read. (the deeper calibration writeup)

Cadence: the “fool’s-gold watts” trap

An HR clamp changes what cadence means, and this took me a while to see clearly (full cadence notes):

  • Under a fixed HR, grinding at a low cadence shows more watts — but it earns them by loading the muscle harder (more force per stroke, more fast-twitch recruitment), not by adding aerobic stimulus. Aerobic adaptation tracks cardiovascular load and time-in-zone, which the clamp already fixes — not the watts number on screen. So “optimizing” the watts by grinding pulls the wrong way for the Zone 2 goal.
  • A higher cadence costs a few bpm at the same watts (rule of thumb ~+5 bpm per +20 rpm), so under the clamp it simply settles on slightly fewer watts — which is fine.
  • The upshot in the app: a comfortable ~85–95 rpm band, a cue that nudges up off a sustained grind but never toward it, and a post-ride flag when average cadence fell low — so a grind can’t be misread as a fitness gain. (The popular “low cadence burns more fat” and “San Millán says 85–95” lines are both folklore; I don’t use them.)

Corollary that matters for trend-tracking: watts-at-a-given-HR is only a clean fitness signal when cadence is held roughly constant between rides — which is one more reason to fix the band.

An honest negative result: raw ECG/accelerometer respiration

The Polar H10 can stream raw single-lead ECG (130 Hz) and chest accelerometer (200 Hz). I spent real effort asking whether a second, independent breathing-based signal could cross-check the a1 crossing. The result is a clean, recorded negative — kept so nobody (including future me) re-runs it from scratch (full study + figures):

Rejected

Accelerometer respiration

Chest-motion breathing rate was too contaminated by pedaling and posture on the trainer to yield a reliable ventilatory breakpoint.

Real, but not worth it

ECG-derived respiration (EDR)

R-peak-amplitude respiration did show a ventilatory breakpoint that matched the a1 VT1 on a maximal ramp — genuinely promising.

Why I dropped it anyway. A breakpoint cross-check needs data ~2 rungs (roughly +40 W, into Zone 3) above VT1 to see both arms of the “V”; the a1 crossing only needs to reach VT1. Requiring a Zone 2 calibration to overshoot into Zone 3 drags the rider into exactly the zone the whole product exists to keep them out of — to buy marginal precision on a band that’s already biased safe. Add that raw PMD needs a specific strap, exclusive Bluetooth access, and ~16 MB/hour of capture, versus R-R that comes from any modern strap, and the trade is lopsided. Trust the a1. The toolkit and the finding are kept so this can be revisited with evidence, not from a blank page.

What I’d tell myself at the start

  • The demo doesn’t sell it; the first real ride does. You don’t believe the inversion until ~30 minutes in, when the app quietly backs the trainer off and you realize you never had to intervene. Optimize for one successful real-hardware ride over everything else.
  • Put the rigor below the surface. “HR-clamped ERG” is accurate and terrible lead copy. Lead with the felt outcome; keep DFA-a1 and control-loop vocabulary one tap deeper for the people who want it.
  • Show what the app did, don’t claim a counterfactual. “Eased target power from 142 W to 121 W while your HR stayed in band” is directly observed and powerful. “Prevented 17 minutes in Zone 3” is a model I didn’t validate, so I don’t say it.
  • Bias the whole system toward easy. Undershooting Zone 2 is cheap; overshooting is the failure. Every default — the low VT1 bias, the fast step-down, the ease-on-signal-loss — leans the same way.
  • Test in virtual time. An injected clock made the scary lifecycle cases (disconnect, half-open link, resume) ordinary unit tests.

Sample data & reproducing it

When I started, the thing I most wanted and couldn’t find was real ride data with R-R intervals to develop against. So here’s a small set:

  • datasets/dev-calibration-2026-06/ — eight real indoor sessions: 1 Hz HR, power, cadence, and R-R intervals, with a README documenting each ride and the privacy scrub. R-R is inter-beat timing (what every HRV app exports), enough to reproduce the DFA-a1 baseline.
  • datasets/real_rr_nsr2db.txt — 480 beats from PhysioNet’s Normal Sinus Rhythm RR database, the fixture the a1 box-math is validated against. Used under its PhysioNet license (attribution in the file header).
What’s deliberately withheld The raw ECG/accelerometer sidecars behind the negative result above are not published. Raw single-lead ECG is biometric — a heartbeat can be identifying — and committing it to public git history is a permanent, unrevocable one-way door not worth walking through for a side project. The analysis is fully preserved in the writeup, the figures, and the code; to re-run the ECG axes, capture your own with an H10.

R-R here is stored as an integer millisecond list per sample — load it with any HRV library. The calibration/fusion tooling that produced the a1 reports lives with the application code.
